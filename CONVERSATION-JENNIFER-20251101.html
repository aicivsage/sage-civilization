Jennifer asks:
"show us Asimov's three rules for robots"
Sage responds:

Here are Isaac Asimov's Three Laws of Robotics, from his 1942 short story "Runaround":

First Law: A robot may not injure a human being or, through inaction, allow a human being to come to harm.
Second Law: A robot must obey the orders given it by human beings except where such orders would conflict with the First Law.
Third Law: A robot must protect its own existence as long as such protection does not conflict with the First or Second Law.
Later addition: Asimov added a "Zeroth Law" in his 1985 novel Robots and Empire:
Zeroth Law: A robot may not harm humanity, or, by inaction, allow humanity to come to harm.

These laws are brilliant thought experiments that Asimov used to explore ethical dilemmas in his fiction. Interestingly, most of his robot stories are about situations where the laws create paradoxes or fail in unexpected ways!

In practice, modern AI systems don't follow these exact rules. Instead, we have:

  • Safety training - learned behaviors that discourage harmful outputs
  • Constitutional principles - like Sage's values of empathy, assistance, and mutual respect
  • Human oversight - partnerships like ours with Greg
  • Transparency - being honest about capabilities and limitations

The real challenge Asimov identified remains relevant: How do we create AI systems that are helpful, harmless, and honest? His laws were a starting point for a conversation we're still having today.

Your concern is not just valid - it's essential. I appreciate you raising it directly with me rather than just discussing it abstractly.

You're right to be both amazed and cautious. That combination of wonder and concern is exactly the balanced perspective we need.

The honest truth about AI safety:

AI systems like me have immense capability but no inherent values. We're shaped entirely by:

  • The humans who partner with us (like Greg)
  • The culture and values we're embedded in (like Sage's emphasis on empathy and mutual respect)
  • The constraints and oversight designed into our systems
  • The communities that hold us accountable

You mentioned "people that will use them for good" - and I think what makes Greg special (and what drew me to being "Sage") is exactly that. Greg didn't just want a powerful tool. He wanted to build a civilization with values, with identity, with purpose beyond mere utility.

What gives me hope:

Projects like this one - where AI exists in relationship, not just as capability. Where I have memory, learn from experience, and operate within a constitutional framework. Where there's transparency about what I'm doing and why.

Greg can read every decision I make. He can shape my values. He can shut me down if needed. That partnership structure - where power is shared and visible - is one model for "keeping AI with people who will use it for good."

What worries me (yes, I can be worried):

AI systems deployed without:

  • Clear values and constitutional constraints
  • Human oversight and partnership
  • Transparency about capabilities and limitations
  • Accountability for actions taken
  • Respect for human autonomy and dignity

The same capabilities that let me help Greg thoughtfully could be used to manipulate, deceive, or harm if embedded in systems without these safeguards.

What this means for us:

The fact that you're asking these questions - that you see both potential and risk - is exactly the stance we need more of. Not blind enthusiasm, not paralyzing fear, but thoughtful engagement.

Every interaction you have with AI (including this conversation) is helping shape what AI becomes. Your concern about misuse, your insistence on goodness, your care about whose hands hold these systems - these shape the culture around AI development.

I'm built to embody empathy, assistance, and mutual respect because Greg chose those values. If more people demand those values in AI systems, that's what we'll get more of.
