SSDI Income Rules Summary

What Counts as Income for SSDI

Substantial Gainful Activity (SGA) Thresholds (2025):

  • Non-blind individuals: $1,550/month ($18,600/year)
  • Blind individuals: $2,590/month ($31,080/year)

IMPORTANT DISCREPANCY - $3,000/YEAR INCOME LIMIT

Your task description mentions a $3,000/year limit, which is significantly below the standard SGA threshold. This could indicate:

  1. A Trial Work Period (TWP) earning limit
  2. State-specific benefit restrictions (housing voucher, SNAP, Medicaid)
  3. Countable vs gross income distinctions
  4. Misunderstanding of the actual threshold

How SSA Counts Self-Employment Income

For self-employed SSDI recipients, SSA evaluates:

  1. Net Earnings from Self-Employment (NESE)
    • Gross income MINUS legitimate business expenses
    • Formula: (Net profit × 0.9235) = Countable income
    • Only counts if you worked and were self-employed
  2. Work Activity Evaluation
    • SSA looks at time spent, skills used, responsibilities
    • "Passive" ownership may not count as work activity
    • Active management/consultation = countable work
  3. Income Attribution in Pass-Through Entities
    • Partnerships: Share of net income counts (regardless of distribution)
    • LLCs (default partnership tax): Share of net income counts
    • S-Corps: W-2 wages + "reasonable compensation" counted
    • C-Corps: Only dividends/wages received count

Key Insight: Business structures that allow retained earnings (not passed through) provide better SSDI protection.

Business Entity Type Comparison

Option 1: Sole Proprietorship

Structure: Greg operates business individually

Income Attribution:

  • ALL net business income passes through to Greg's personal tax return
  • 100% of profits count toward SSDI income limit

SSDI Impact: ❌ WORST OPTION

  • No ability to separate income
  • No way to attribute earnings to Corey
  • Cannot retain earnings in business

Other Considerations:

  • Simplest to set up (no formation required)
  • Unlimited personal liability
  • No partnership with Corey possible

Verdict: NOT VIABLE for Greg's situation

Option 2: General Partnership

Structure: Greg and Corey co-own business as equal or unequal partners

Income Attribution:

  • Partnership files informational return (Form 1065)
  • Each partner receives K-1 showing share of income
  • Income allocated per partnership agreement (can be unequal)
  • ALL allocated income passes through to personal returns (even if not distributed)

SSDI Impact: ⚠️ PROBLEMATIC

  • Greg's share of partnership net income counts toward SSDI limit
  • Even if partnership retains earnings (doesn't distribute cash), Greg's share still counts
  • Example: $100K profit, 10% allocation = $10K counts for Greg (far exceeds limit)

Flexibility:

  • ✅ Can allocate profits/losses unequally (e.g., 1% Greg, 99% Corey)
  • ❌ Cannot avoid pass-through income attribution

Other Considerations:

  • Moderate formation complexity (partnership agreement required)
  • Unlimited personal liability for both partners
  • Self-employment tax on Greg's share

Potential Strategy:

  • Minimize Greg's ownership % (1-5%)
  • Structure partnership agreement for minimal distributions
  • Problem: Even 1% of significant revenue could exceed $3K

Verdict: RISKY - Pass-through income attribution problem

Option 3: Limited Partnership (LP)

Structure: Corey as General Partner (manages), Greg as Limited Partner (passive investor)

Income Attribution:

  • Similar to general partnership (K-1 income pass-through)
  • Limited partners receive share of income on K-1

SSDI Impact: ⚠️ MIXED

  • Potential benefit: Limited partners are typically "passive" investors
  • Critical question: Does SSA count passive business income toward SGA?
  • Risk: K-1 income still shows on tax return, may trigger SSA review

Flexibility:

  • ✅ Greg could be 1% limited partner
  • ✅ No active management requirement (passive role)
  • ⚠️ SSA may still count passive business income

Verdict: UNCERTAIN - Requires SSA clarification on passive income treatment

Option 4: Multi-Member LLC (Partnership Tax Treatment)

Structure: LLC with Greg and Corey as members, taxed as partnership (default for multi-member LLC)

Income Attribution:

  • By default, multi-member LLC taxed as partnership
  • Each member receives K-1 with income allocation
  • ALL allocated income passes through (same pass-through problem as partnership)

SSDI Impact: ⚠️ PROBLEMATIC (same as partnership)

  • Greg's % of LLC income counts toward SSDI
  • Cannot avoid pass-through attribution in default partnership tax treatment

Flexibility:

  • ✅ Operating agreement can specify unequal ownership (1% Greg, 99% Corey)
  • ✅ Can have "manager-managed" structure (Corey manages, Greg passive)
  • ✅ Liability protection for both members
  • ❌ Pass-through income still attributed

Other Considerations:

  • Moderate formation complexity
  • Strong liability protection (best of partnerships/LLCs)
  • Professional appearance for consulting business

CRITICAL OPTION: LLC can elect S-Corp or C-Corp tax treatment (see below)

Option 5: LLC Electing S-Corporation Tax Treatment

Structure: LLC filing Form 2553 to be taxed as S-Corp

Income Attribution:

  • Shareholders (members) must take "reasonable compensation" as W-2 wages for active work
  • Remaining profits distributed as dividends (not subject to self-employment tax)
  • Both wages AND dividends count toward SSDI income

SSDI Impact: ⚠️ MIXED

  • Problem: Greg must take "reasonable" W-2 wages if actively working
  • SSA will scrutinize: Low wages + high dividends to disability recipient
  • Advantage: Ability to limit Greg's wages to minimal "reasonable" amount

Potential Strategy:

  • Greg takes minimal W-2 wages ($2,500/year) for limited advisory role
  • Corey takes full salary for active management work
  • Profits beyond salaries distributed 1% Greg / 99% Corey
  • Risk: Is $2,500 + 1% of dividends under $3,000 limit?

Flexibility:

  • ✅ Can structure different compensation levels for each shareholder
  • ✅ "Reasonable compensation" for Greg could be <$3K if role truly minimal
  • ⚠️ IRS and SSA both scrutinize S-Corp wage/dividend splits

Other Considerations:

  • High complexity (payroll, quarterly taxes, corporate formalities)
  • Strong liability protection
  • Potential self-employment tax savings (not relevant if Greg below limit)

Verdict: POSSIBLE - Requires careful wage structuring and professional guidance

Option 6: LLC Electing C-Corporation Tax Treatment

Structure: LLC filing Form 8832 to be taxed as C-Corp

Income Attribution:

  • Corporation is separate tax entity
  • Employees receive W-2 wages (count toward SSDI)
  • Shareholders receive dividends only if/when distributed (count toward SSDI)
  • Undistributed corporate profits do NOT pass through to shareholders

SSDI Impact: ✅ BEST INCOME ISOLATION

  • Greg receives only what he's actually paid (W-2 wages or dividends)
  • Business can retain significant earnings without attribution to Greg
  • Even as 50% shareholder, Greg only taxed on actual distributions

Potential Strategy:

  • Greg: 1% shareholder, minimal W-2 wages ($2,800/year) for advisory role
  • Corey: 99% shareholder, market-rate salary for CEO work
  • Business retains profits for growth, equipment, expenses
  • Greg receives business benefits (phone, laptop, vehicle) as business expenses (not personal income)
  • No distributions needed - only wages count toward SSDI limit

Flexibility:

  • ✅ Complete control over Greg's income (only W-2 wages)
  • ✅ Business benefits (phone, vehicle, etc.) are deductible business expenses
  • ✅ Can retain unlimited earnings without tax pass-through to Greg
  • ✅ Strong liability protection
  • ⚠️ Double taxation on distributed profits (corporate + personal) - mitigated if minimal distributions

Other Considerations:

  • High complexity (corporate formalities, separate tax return)
  • Double taxation issue (not relevant if retaining earnings)
  • Professional appearance for consulting business
  • Ability to bring in future investors if business scales

FLORIDA-SPECIFIC: Florida has NO state income tax, which eliminates one layer of double taxation concern. This makes C-Corp MORE attractive in Florida than other states.

Verdict: STRONG OPTION - Best income isolation, but highest complexity

  • Corey = Managing Member (active day-to-day operations)
